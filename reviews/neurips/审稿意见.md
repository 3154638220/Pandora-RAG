**Summary:**

The authors present Pandora-RAG, an adaptive stopping framework for multi-hop retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems. The proposed method models iterative retrieval as a finite-horizon optimal stopping problem. It relies on a Bellman oracle derived from fully rolled-out trajectories to generate supervision labels for a deployable neural probe. This probe decides whether to stop or continue retrieval based on LLM hidden states and observable shallow features. Additionally, the authors introduce an E-value process to monitor the stopped-answer stream for anytime-valid risk evidence in delayed-feedback deployments.

**Contribution Type:** General: Most submissions will fall into this type.

**Strengths And Weaknesses:**

Strengths

Theoretical Grounding: The framing of iterative RAG stopping as a finite-horizon optimal stopping problem is theoretically sound, and the separation of the structural upper bound (the oracle) from the deployable proxy is well-reasoned.

Online Monitoring: Introducing an outcome-aware E-value monitor provides an interpretable, anytime-valid risk evidence trajectory to detect degraded streams.

Efficiency: For simpler datasets like HotpotQA, the learned probe reduces average retrieval steps significantly while maintaining a strong Pareto tradeoff compared to fixed-depth baselines.

Weaknesses

1. Distribution Sensitivity of the Deployable Probe The most vulnerable assumption in this work is that the current hidden state and shallow retrieval features can predict the value of future retrieval well enough under new distributions. If this assumption fails, the Bellman oracle remains mathematically valid, but the deployable probe stops in the wrong places. The paper inadvertently provides evidence that the learned stopping rule is highly distribution-sensitive. The authors admit that cross-dataset threshold transfer is weak, acknowledging that new domains require their own trajectory rollouts and tuning. Furthermore, on the more complex MuSiQue benchmark, the probe recovers only 77.9% of the oracle F1 while over-retrieving compared to the oracle, demonstrating that the probe struggles when reasoning paths become harder and the base error rate increases.
2. Susceptibility to Confident Distractors Because the probe relies heavily on internal states and shallow confidence markers, the method risks learning confidence-shaped shortcuts rather than genuine answer completeness. A critical failure mode exists: questions where the first retrieved document provides a confident but incorrect answer, while the correct bridge evidence appears only in later retrieval steps. In such scenarios, the model's hidden state would look settled early, causing Pandora-RAG's probe to stop prematurely, whereas a fixed, deeper retrieval strategy would succeed.
3. Outdated Intuitions and Missing Agentic Baselines The paper bases its core motivation on the observation that fixed-depth RAG is wasteful on easy questions and insufficient on hard ones. If full rollouts are collected during training, trajectories reveal where stopping is best—suggesting a dynamic-programming oracle. However, this intuition is anchored to an outdated premise of how multi-hop reasoning is currently handled. The authors evaluate exclusively against fixed-depth pipelines and Stop-RAG, completely omitting current state-of-the-art agentic RAG methods. Recent workflows have demonstrated that coding agents are effective long-context processors capable of managing retrieval tasks dynamically, reasoning through missing information, and executing multi-hop tool calls without being bottlenecked by rigid iterative depth. By not discussing or comparing against agentic frameworks, the paper fails to situate its contributions within the current frontier of adaptive retrieval.
4. Decorative Theoretical Results The theoretical contributions appear to be largely decorative and lack substantial novelty. Proposition A.1 is a textbook instance of finite-horizon dynamic programming. Proposition A.2, with the expectation dropped, is a trivial argmax over a known F1 sequence, which does not address stopping under uncertainty. Furthermore, Proposition A.3 relies on a standard betting-supermartingale and Ville inequality argument. None of these represent a real theoretical contribution to the field.
5. Disconnect Between Theory and the Deployed Method Proposition A.1 is the only result modeling uncertainty via . However, the actual pipeline never estimates this conditional expectation. Instead, Stage 1 of the method utilizes the hindsight per-trajectory  from Proposition A.2. Consequently, the "optimal-stopping formulation" is merely realized by a hindsight argmax, leaving Proposition A.1 as a hanging theorem that does not mathematically ground the deployed method.
6. Unlearnable Target and Missing Link to Policy The continuation label relies on future F1 scores and is not -measurable, whereas the neural probe only observes  during deployment. This presents a fundamental measurability obstruction rather than a simple approximation error. The paper lacks any regret, consistency, or admissibility bound tying the deployed policy  to the optimal value . By Jensen's inequality, the oracle over-estimates , rendering the "upper bound" a loose hindsight bound. This theoretical gap is empirically consistent with the observation that the probe never beats the fixed-depth baselines on F1.
7. Over-formalization Decoupled from Experiments The framing around Pandora's Box and Weitzman's rule is largely inapplicable to this setting. By the authors' own admission, multi-hop retrieval violates the independent-box assumption, and reservation values are never instantiated in the deployable method. **Summary:**

This paper studies adaptive stopping for iterative multi-hop RAG. The proposed Pandora-RAG framework formulates stopping along a fixed retrieval trajectory as a finite-horizon optimal stopping problem. It constructs a Bellman oracle from fully rolled-out trajectories, trains a deployable neural probe using LLM hidden states and observable retrieval features, and adds an E-value process to monitor the adaptively stopped answer stream when delayed outcome feedback becomes available. The method is evaluated on HotpotQA, MuSiQue, and 2WikiMultiHopQA and compared against Stop-RAG in an aligned diagnostic setup.

**Contribution Type:** General: Most submissions will fall into this type.

**Strengths And Weaknesses:**

Strengths:

1. The problem is important and well motivated. Iterative retrieval has a real cost-quality trade-off, and fixed-depth retrieval is inefficient for heterogeneous multi-hop questions.
2. The separation between an offline Bellman oracle and a deployable stopping probe is conceptually clean. It provides an interpretable upper bound for the realized trajectory and a principled source of supervision.
3. The E-value monitoring component is a useful addition. It addresses a deployment concern that offline F1 alone cannot capture: the stopped answer stream may degrade under distribution shift.
4. The comparison against Stop-RAG is relevant, and the matched online stopping constraints make the empirical comparison more meaningful than a purely offline replay comparison.

Weaknesses:

1. The oracle is trajectory-specific and depends on fully rolled-out future answer quality. This is reasonable for supervision, but the resulting upper bound is only relative to the chosen retriever/generator trajectory, not to the broader space of retrieval actions.
2. Threshold selection appears dataset-specific and requires labeled development data. The paper notes weak cross-dataset transfer, which limits immediate deployment in new domains.
3. The probe depends on hidden-state access, so the method is less applicable to closed-source LLM APIs or commercial RAG stacks where internal representations are unavailable.
4. The monitoring component requires delayed outcome feedback from labels, audits, or reviews. The paper should clarify how frequently such feedback is needed to obtain useful alerts in realistic deployments.
5. The empirical section would be stronger with more baselines beyond Stop-RAG and simple thresholding, including uncertainty-based stopping, self-consistency-based stopping, and cost-aware routing variants adapted to the same backbone.

**Quality:** 3: good

**Clarity:** 3: good

**Significance:** 3: good

**Originality:** 4: excellent

**Questions:**

1. How does the learned stopping policy behave when the retriever returns highly redundant or low-quality evidence across steps?
2. How sensitive are the results to the per-step cost parameter?
3. Can the method be adapted to closed-weight models using only logprobs or document-level features?
4. How much labeled rollout data is required before the probe becomes reliable on a new domain?
5. What is the practical audit rate needed for the E-value monitor to detect degradation with useful delay?

**Limitations:**

No.

1. I suggest expand the probe input from LLM hidden states to document-level features, for compatibility to commercial close-weight model. Due to this limitation, I'm concerned about the practicality of this technology, although I like the overall technical direction presented by this paper.
2. I suggest expand the risk detection to label free risk detection for broader use, which currently limits the practical use in real-world.
